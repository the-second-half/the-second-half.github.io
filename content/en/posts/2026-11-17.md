Subtitle: Why did something that took eight months keep failing for the previous thirty years?

From February to the end of October 2026 — eight months — I went from 95.5 kg to 74.8 kg, forty jin lost. That part is easy to tell; the last chapter laid out all three meals a day. What does not add up is this: the same person, for roughly thirty years before that, never once succeeded at losing weight. Not for lack of starting. Every start simply did not count — either I stopped halfway, or the weight came back. Most people around me are the same: losing weight over and over, year after year, the curve looking like a saw blade.

There is one more thing I keep thinking about: there are no fat people in prison. Humanitarian questions set aside for a moment, in that kind of highly closed collective living environment you hardly see a fat person either. The two things look unrelated. They are actually the same answer.

### 1. Fatness is not a cause, it is a result

We are used to treating fatness as a fault to be fixed: it sits there in the way, just move it. So all the effort goes into the moving.

I see it the other way round. Fatness is a result — the result of a problem in the body's whole energy balance and metabolic system. How much goes in, how much is spent, whether you slept enough, how much stress you are under, what state the liver and thyroid are in: together these decide how much surplus energy the body stores as fat. Weight is only the last number in that account.

Weight loss is a result too, something that happens along the way once the metabolic system has rebalanced. The layer before it cannot be skipped. Go straight for the last number and even if you get it, you cannot keep it.

### 2. The goal is health, not a smaller number

If the goal of losing weight is a falling number on the scale, or looking "thin", the target was wrong from the start. Being thin is a result too, not a goal.

What I actually watched this time was not the scale. It was blood lipids, liver function, blood sugar, HbA1c, fatty liver; TSH coming down from 10.74 to 4.09 and entering the reference range for the first time; triglycerides at 4.56 → 2.32 → 0.83 across three tests. The weight came down alongside all of that. When the number did not move for days during those eight months, what mattered more to me was that the body was telling me some link had fallen behind — not that the scale had lost a few tenths of a kilo.

So any method that singles out "lower body weight" as the goal is, to me, putting the cart before the horse. Eat a little less, move a little more, and the number looks good that same day — but the metabolic system has not moved and the debt is still on the books. As for pressing that number down with drugs, my own choice is not to take that road: the weight is being held down while life and metabolism have not changed at all, and I am not sure what I would face after stopping. That is a personal choice, not medication advice; if you are ill and need medication, listen to your doctor.

### 3. If it is a result, fat loss is a systems job

Change fat loss from "one action" into "one result" and the method changes immediately.

Everything connected to metabolism affects the outcome: food, exercise, sleep, water, mood, lifestyle, and also medication (where it is necessary), preventing complications, limiting damage. These are not parallel options. They are different ports on the same system; block any one of them and it leaks out somewhere else.

My own records show it clearly. After the fistula surgery on 23 September, weight rebounded to 78.55 and took a month to come back to 74.8; from August my hair thinned noticeably; for a while constipation came only once every three or four days. The eating was right, but water was short, carbohydrate was cut too hard, sleep was poor — and the body collected the debt elsewhere. Look only at the calorie deficit and none of this makes sense. Put it into the system and all of it does.

When the number sat still for ten-odd days, what people call a plateau, I stopped treating it as an enemy. The system was finding a new balance point. It was not that I was not trying hard enough.

### 4. The first principle: build a healthy-life shield

So what is the first thing, underneath everything else?

My answer: make yourself a living pattern that is as closed as possible and that favours fat loss and the return of health. I call it the healthy-life shield. It is not discipline and it is not willpower; it is an environment. When the environment is relatively closed, the damage outside cannot seep in, and you do not have to fight it every single day.

Concretely:

1. Eat at home. Buy the ingredients yourself; boil, steam, simple cold dishes — twenty minutes a meal. Nothing unclear gets into the shield.
2. Cut back work banquets. Strictly no alcohol, not a drop of any alcoholic drink. One drink you cannot refuse at work can undo a month of effort — not because of those calories, but because it opens the shield.
3. No commercial drinks of any kind, strict sugar control, no cakes, durian or mango.
4. Sleep on time, no food after 7 p.m., at least 2000 ml of water a day, a glass first thing in the morning.
5. Do not overdo exercise: mainly brisk walking, 7000 to 9000 steps a day on average, no running. Over-exercising is damage too, and it counts in the system.
6. Keep mood and routine as steady as possible, weigh and record every day, and when things slip, get back on track the next day without self-reproach.

### 5. Why there are no fat people in a closed system

Back to the question at the start.

In prison, and in those highly closed collective living environments, what you eat, when you eat, when you sleep, how much you move, what you can get hold of — none of it is decided on the spot by the individual. Humanitarian questions aside, that is an extreme, closed living system in which getting fat is almost impossible: no takeout, no banquets, no late-night drink, no spur-of-the-moment "just this once". People inside do not need willpower. It would be hard not to get thin.

This is not praise for that kind of environment. It is to peel out one thing: what decides weight is not a few choices, but whether the system allows you to get fat. What we actually want to copy is its closedness, nothing else.

### 6. Why repeated attempts at losing weight keep failing

That answers the third question too.

People lose weight over and over, quit halfway or rebound — not because they lack methods, and not because their willpower is too soft, but because the healthy-life shield was never built. As long as that system is not fully established, success is close to impossible: you are relying on resisting in the moment, and resistance in the moment will fail one day.

The reverse is also true. Break the shield after succeeding, go back to your old way of living, and rebound is inevitable. The body is not holding a grudge; the system has been switched back, so the result switches back with it. My own 2.8 kg rebound in September was exactly that — surgery, meals out, not enough water, a hole in the shield.

So "no rebound" was never a promise. It is a structural question: is the shield still there?

### 7. Conclusion

Fat loss is the result of the body returning to normal metabolism, and normal metabolism is the result of a long-term, correct living environment. The layer in between cannot be skipped.

Do not fixate on the number on the scale. Build the healthy living system first, keep it as closed as you can for as long as you can, block what can be blocked, and where you cannot, keep a record and come back the next day. With the system in place, fat loss comes along with it, and the effort goes twice as far.

What I did in those eight months, in the end, was this one thing.

(This is a personal measured record and a lifestyle practice, not a treatment plan.)
