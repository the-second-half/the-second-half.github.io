### 1. The numbers first

In February 2026 I put the whole life-management plan into practice. By the end of October — eight months — my weight went from a worst-ever 95.5 kg down to 74.8 kg at its lowest. Forty jin. That is what 2026 has been.

It was not only weight. Blood lipids, liver function, blood sugar, HbA1c, fatty liver — one by one they moved back, and most of the red marks on the reports were erased. The thyroid was the stubborn one: TSH fell from 10.74 to 4.09, inside the reference range for the first time; the antibody TPOAb fell from 267 to 158, a drop of forty per cent, though still above normal. All of that has been written up in earlier chapters. Here I will only say: eat right, and the body pays its debts back one entry at a time.

The fall was even. From a May monthly mean of 86.3 kg to 76.0 kg in October, 1.6 to 2.6 kg a month, no cliff and no rebound. The fistula surgery on 23 September was the single break — weight went back to 78.55 after it, then fell to 74.8 within a month, below the pre-surgery low. The body follows a long trend, not a single day.

### 2. The base of this way of eating: no hunger, no gaps, no willpower

Many people think losing weight means enduring. My eight months were not endured: three meals a day as usual, protein in full, nutrients backed by food plus a few supplements, and not one night going to bed hungry.

More to the point, it does not depend on willpower. There was no daily gritting of teeth against appetite, because hunger itself was held down by the structure of the food. Nor did it interfere with life and work — business trips, meals out and gatherings all happened, and there were days that ran off the rails (an estimated 2900 kcal on 22 October, eating out). The next day I went back on track, and the weight curve did not turn around.

### 3. Eat right, and eighty per cent is done

The key to fat loss is controlling what you eat every day. Looking at my own numbers again and again, the conclusion is blunt: eat wrong and fat loss basically fails; eat right and eighty per cent of it is done. The other twenty per cent is exercise, sleep and mindset.

Food also affects sleep quality indirectly. Eat too much, too late, or drink the wrong thing in the evening, and sleep turns bad at once; bad sleep leaves appetite and mood unstable the next day, and the vicious circle starts. So controlling the mouth controls sleep too.

### 4. Never lean on starvation

By starvation I mean the long-term hungry kind of eating less. Never lean on it, for two reasons.

(1) When the calorie gap is too large, the body sharply lowers its basal metabolic rate. This is a protection mechanism millions of years old: the body decides there is a famine and turns the furnace down. Once basal metabolism falls, every further kilo is harder, and what is lost is not only fat but muscle. Use food to "trick" the body into believing there is no famine, to keep basal metabolism at a normal level — that is the only way fat loss can continue.

(2) Willpower is a consumable. Anyone who stays hungry long enough will collapse sooner or later, and after the collapse comes rebound eating, and the whole process stops, with the weight coming back doubled. I have seen too many people like that, and I came close myself. A designed way of eating, not willpower held by force, is what carried me through eight months.

### 5. Clean food, clean drink: the first principle

Clean means no additives. Any additive — salt, sugars, preservatives, seasonings — is calories, and is harmful to the body. They are calorie assassins: hidden inside processed food that looks harmless, pushing calories up without your noticing, and stimulating appetite so you eat more.

The same goes for drink. Plain water and lightly brewed tea are fine; with everything else in a bottle or a can, assume it is not clean.

### 6. To eat clean, you must cook

Every restaurant, every takeaway, every cooked-food counter adds large amounts of seasoning and preservatives — without heavy flavour customers do not come back, and without additives the food will not keep for three days. That is the logic of the trade, not a fault of one particular shop.

So cooking for yourself is a necessity, not a refinement. Buy the ingredients for the week, boil, steam, dress simply — twenty minutes a meal. If you genuinely cannot cook and cannot guarantee clean food, fat loss will be very hard. I will not gloss over that.

### 7. Only after clean comes how much and what

The order cannot be reversed: settle clean first, then talk about quantity and variety. With unclean food, controlling quantity means nothing, because the additives are already quietly wrecking your calorie ledger and your appetite.

### 8. What I do not eat

(1) Anything I cannot identify. Takeaway is the example — no idea what is in it or what the raw materials were. I do not touch it.

(2) No alcohol at all. Not a drop of any alcoholic drink.

(3) No commercial drinks of any kind. However healthy the packaging claims to be — zero sugar, pure juice, probiotic — I do not drink it.

(4) No fatty meat. Chicken skin, pork skin, slabs of fat — none of it.

(5) Never a full dinner. The evening meal keeps me alive; it is not for pleasure.

(6) Strict sugar control. Nothing high in sugar: durian, mango, and every kind of pastry. Sugar does the most direct damage to a middle-aged metabolism, so this one gets its own line.

### 9. The basic principle: a calorie gap

Losing fat means creating a calorie gap every day. There is no way around that.

For the long haul the gap must exist, but must not be too large. Without a gap there is no fat loss; with too large a gap come hunger and malnutrition. My own sprint phase had a large gap — maintenance around 2156 kcal while median intake was only a little over 1100 — workable in the short term, but it costs, and hair loss and constipation were part of the cost. After moving into the holding phase I brought the gap back to a gentle 10 to 20 per cent: no hunger, a slow decline, or simply holding steady.

Beyond total calories comes balance among the nutrients: protein, fat, carbohydrate, fibre. My rules — better too much protein than too little; better too little fat than too much; coarse carbohydrate rather than refined; fibre at every meal. And water in full, better too much than too little.

### 10. Water: the accelerant and the lubricant

It is hard to overstate water. Fat breakdown needs it, waste removal needs it, constipation prevention needs it. With too little water the machine grinds dry. I take more than two litres a day: a glass on waking, a glass before meals, a glass before and after exercise.

### 11. When to eat and drink, and how to spread the calories

When you eat matters as much as what: three fixed points.

Protein in full in the morning — eggs, milk, oats. That is the first supply after a night of spending, and the anchor of a stable appetite.

Put the hard-to-digest food at midday. Red meat and anything heavy gets a whole afternoon to be dealt with.

Eat less at night; after seven in the evening, no food and only a little water. Going to bed slightly hungry actually sleeps better; going to bed full guarantees an ugly number on the scale next morning.

### 12. Supplements: what the body needs, less rather than more

Food is the base; supplements only fill gaps. The principle: supplement what the body actually needs, have it if needed, less rather than more. Never pile up varieties or doses — an excess of vitamins is also a burden. Every item I take has a clear reason, listed in the next section; not one of them is there because I "heard it was good".

### 13. What I eat: the food list

Eaten heavily (ten or more times a month, from highest frequency down):

(1) Eggs: about 23 times a month, one or two per meal, boiled. The base of breakfast.

(2) Green leafy vegetables: about 18 times a month, 200 g a serving, boiled.

(3) Skimmed milk: about 16 times a month, 200 ml at a time.

(4) Rolled oats: about 14 times a month, 30 to 80 g a meal.

(5) Firm tofu: about 11 times a month, 250 to 300 g at a time.

The standing fruit list: blueberries, pomelo, cucumber, cherry tomatoes, carrots — nutrition with low sugar. Pomelo most often; cucumber, cherry tomatoes and carrots as raw snacks.

Nuts every day: raw sweet almonds, 5 to 10 at a time; raw pumpkin seed kernels, a small handful. Mid-morning or mid-afternoon, always raw and unprocessed. A little nuts daily supplies magnesium and zinc, and also fat and protein.

Eaten occasionally (under ten times a month): salmon, baby pumpkin, potato, chicken breast, tomato, prune, taro, white mushroom, tofu skin, king oyster mushroom, okra, beef, shiitake. The only cooking oil is olive oil, one tablespoon to dress boiled vegetables; most meals have none at all.

### 14. Supplements for my own conditions

I am fifty, and the problems that come with being overweight are ones many people my age share. I have Hashimoto's thyroiditis, which has progressed to clinical hypothyroidism, but I insist on not taking levothyroxine and regulate thyroid function through food and lifestyle instead — a few items on the list are aimed at exactly that. My supplement list is below (a personal record, not medical advice):

(1) Multivitamin, one tablet a day, after breakfast — a general backstop.

(2) Vitamin D, 400 IU a day, after breakfast — widely low at my age, and Hashimoto's patients need it more.

(3) Selenium yeast, 50 mcg raised to 100 mcg, added this August — for Hashimoto's thyroiditis. From late October I switched to selenium-enriched eggs and stopped the tablet.

(4) Zinc gluconate oral liquid, 20 ml twice a day — for hair loss and immunity, kept two hours apart from milk and oats.

(5) Lactulose oral solution, starting at 30 ml and reduced to 10–15 ml — for constipation during fat loss.

### 16. What I would tell you

(1) Losing fat and holding weight are a lifetime task. It is never too late to start, and the earlier the better for the body. I started at fifty, and it was still in time.

(2) Fat loss is a system. Food, exercise, sleep, mindset — they work as one system. Do not expect a single measure to reach the goal, and do not lean on drugs.

(3) Mindset matters; do not be in a hurry. For a large body, give it at least six to twelve months. As long as there is a daily gap, it accumulates and you will get there — my eight months are the proof.

(4) Have regular check-ups: blood pressure, blood lipids, blood sugar, transaminases, thyroid, plus an ultrasound of liver and gallbladder. Once a quarter, and adjust the plan accordingly.

(5) Use AI. Record and ask about your daily state and your food as you go. Of my 941 daily records, more than half were turned into usable data with AI's help. At fifty, let the tools do the labour.

No shore, so I row.
