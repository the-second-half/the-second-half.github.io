Subtitle: Stable weight on a stable system | First half of November health review

This is the first month after my weight came down to the standard range in October. The first days were fine — everything ran to plan and the number kept sliding down. Then the last three days undid the rhythm: a business trip wrecked both my eating and my schedule, and the weight swung hard. I caught it in time; nothing serious came of it.

## 1. The conclusion first: half a month, +0.1 kg

From 74.8 kg on the morning of October 31 to 74.9 kg on the morning of November 15 — a net gain of 0.1 kg in half a month.

What happened in between: one business trip to Shenzhen, two business dinners, two bottles of beer, and half a kilo of dried longan.

I had just finished writing about the "healthy living shield" in Chapter 16. In these two weeks I punched four holes in it myself. And the number on the scale barely moved. That is not luck — it is the metabolic base I built over eight months holding the line for me. How that base was built is in the earlier chapters. Here I only describe what happened in these two weeks.

## 2. Daily weight for the half month

![](/images/ch17-weight-day-en.png)

Two numbers deserve their own sentence.

First, on the morning of November 12 I weighed 74.35 kg — nearly half a kilo below October's low of 74.80. So not only did the half month not add weight, it touched a new low for the year in the middle of it.

Second, from 95.5 kg in February to today is 20.6 kg lost. Against the 70 kg goal, 4.9 kg to go.

## 3. Calories and the gap: over 14 days, the books balance

My baseline is 1700 kcal a day; the gap is intake minus 1700, negative meaning a deficit and positive meaning over. There are no day-by-day food records from October 1 to 21, so the ledger starts on October 22 — which happens to join up with the ten days in Chapter 14.

![](/images/ch17-ledger-en.png)

![](/images/ch17-trend-en.png)

Add up the 14 recorded days from October 31 to November 15: intake 23,653 kcal, baseline 23,800 kcal, a cumulative deficit of 147 kcal — under 11 kcal a day on average.

One line in that ledger is worth spelling out: ten days in deficit, 3,777 kcal in total; four days over, 3,630 kcal in total. The two sides almost exactly cancel. A net gain of 0.1 kg over the half month and a net deficit of 147 kcal are two ways of writing the same fact.

At 7700 kcal per kilogram of fat, 147 kcal is 19 grams. Less than the error bar of the scale.

So the real conclusion is not "the lapses did no harm." It is that the lapses never pulled me out of deficit — the other days absorbed them. Had a few of those ten days slipped, the arithmetic would read differently.

The 7700 kcal figure needs its boundary stated. It is an empirical value Wishnofsky proposed in 1958, valid when everything lost is fat. In reality every kilogram on the scale is mixed with water, glycogen and lean mass. Hall calculated in the International Journal of Obesity in 2008 that the linear conversion holds roughly for the first weeks and then overestimates, because resting expenditure falls as weight falls; in 2011 he and his colleagues published a model in The Lancet showing that each sustained 10 kcal/day reduction eventually yields about 0.45 kg, half of it only after a year. Add the adaptive thermogenesis described by Rosenbaum and Leibel in 2010: a sustained deficit makes the body turn its own expenditure down.

With that in mind, half a month is not worth agonising over: fat changes too little and water and glycogen swing too much. To read fat, do not stare at a single day — watch the two-to-four-week average of morning weights.

My baseline is 1700 kcal a day, 105 g protein, 48 g fat, 175 g carbohydrate, 28 g fibre. In half a month I lapsed four times:

| 11.13–11.14 | Shenzhen trip | Flight delayed, first meal at 3 pm (Meizhou noodles); back-to-back dinners and two bottles of beer; 20 then 10 detox tablets over two days | 75.6 on the morning of the 14th, up 1.2 kg from the 13th |

Four lapses, four entries. I write them down not to forgive myself but so that next time I know where the holes leak.

## 5. Two extreme days: one good, one bad

The good news was the longan day. The morning after the dried longan, November 12, I weighed 74.35 kg — the lightest of the half month. I worked it out afterwards: overall intake those days was low, so a big carbohydrate load did not become fat first; it became liver glycogen and water, and it drained overnight. But that is not a licence to eat freely — a single day means nothing, the sum of the days around it is what counts.

The bad news was the Shenzhen day. On November 14, three restaurant meals and two bottles of beer, intake estimated above 3500 kcal — 105% over baseline, fat above 120 g, sodium badly over. On the morning of the 14th I was 75.6 kg, up 1.2 kg from the day before. Most of that was water and sodium, not flesh: I kept walking hard that day and the next, and by the morning of the 15th I was back at 74.9.

Up 1.2 in a day, back 0.7 in two. That is what weight variation looks like in maintenance. Do not let a single day scare you, and do not let it fool you either.

Many people step on the scale at night and despair, convinced the day was wasted. But being around 1.5 kg heavier at night is normal — that is the weight of food, water and residue. Sleep takes it away through breath, sweat and the toilet. What matters is this morning against last morning: morning to morning is the only comparison that is about fat.

## 7. Putting three numbers together: gap, overnight loss, metabolism

Set "that day's gap" and "that night's loss" side by side and things get interesting.

| Date | Gap that day (kcal) | Loss overnight (kg) | Next-morning change (kg) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 11.03 | +50 | 2.35 | +0.05 |
| 11.05 | −100 | 1.05 | −0.30 |
| 11.09 | −395 | 1.45 | −0.70 |
| 11.11 | +1400 | 1.20 | −0.45 |
| 11.12 | −100 | 1.65 | +0.05 |
| 11.14 | +1800 | 1.60 | −0.70 |

The first finding: the biggest gap did not produce the biggest overnight loss. On November 14 I was 1800 kcal over and lost 1.60 overnight; on November 3 I was only 50 kcal over and lost 2.35, the largest of the half month. On the 1400-over longan night I lost 1.20 and still woke to a new low.

The second finding: across these six pairs the correlation coefficient is −0.13, which is to say no relationship. How much I ate that day and how much I lost that night are two separate books.

That is not a data error; it is physiology. The weight lost overnight comes from roughly these sources:

- Insensible loss through breath and skin: exhaled air at sleep is saturated vapour and skin keeps evaporating, together about 0.3 to 0.5 kg a night;
- Carbon exhaled: the carbon from oxidised fat and sugar leaves the lungs as carbon dioxide, about 0.1 to 0.2 kg a night;
- Night urine: the kidneys do not clock off, and the first morning void is usually 0.3 to 0.5 kg;
- Glycogen and the water bound to it: each gram of glycogen holds about 3 to 4 g of water, and as liver glycogen is broken down overnight the water goes with it.

Those four items add up to about a kilogram, and they have little to do with "I ate a few hundred kcal less today." They have much more to do with how salty dinner was, how much I drank, whether I had a bowel movement, and how dry the bedroom was. The 2.35 on November 3 was the potassium in the pomelo carrying sodium and water out; the 1.60 on November 14 was laxatives and alcohol. Neither had anything to do with fat.

The third finding is the one most often got wrong: overnight loss is not metabolic rate.

Metabolic rate during sleep is 88% to 95% of basal metabolic rate — slightly lower than when awake. Eight hours of sleep burns roughly 400 to 600 kcal; even if every calorie came from fat, that is 50 to 80 g, which no scale can resolve. So using "I lost 1.5 kg last night" as proof of "good metabolism" does not hold up. What it measures is water and salt balance, glycogen stores and gut contents — a cash-flow statement, not a metabolic reading.

So what can serve as a reference for metabolism? Three things:

1. **A multi-day average of morning weights under the same conditions.** Seven-day averages compared with seven-day averages — faster or slower is the real trend;
2. **The weight response to the same intake.** 1500 kcal versus 2500 kcal, and where the weight sits a week later, is that individual's honest answer;
3. **Resting metabolic rate measurement.** Properly done it needs indirect calorimetry; home body-composition scales give a formula estimate, good only for a rough idea.

Back to this half month: a cumulative deficit of −147 kcal and a net gain of 0.1 kg validate each other — the books on intake and expenditure are essentially balanced. How much I lost overnight is a different book. It looks good, but it cannot be used to draw conclusions.

## 8. The wound healed

Fifty-three days since the fistula surgery on September 23. On November 13 the hard scab began to lift in places, with no bleeding and no exudate; on November 14 it came away completely, the surface flat, no pain — grade-A healing.

This is the record I am steadiest about in the whole half month. For more than two months after surgery I could not run or walk long distances; October's loss came almost entirely from eating. With the wound closed, exercise can come back gradually from the second half of November.

## 9. No running, but walking every day

The fistula is still recovering, so I did not run once. My bottom line was that the legs must not idle: keep at least some walking every single day, hold daily activity at a level, and keep the body from going flat. Day-by-day figures for November 1 to 8 (estimates):

For November 9 to 15 the app only gives a weekly summary: 8,687 steps a day on average, 340 kcal of activity a day, 7.7 km a day (the summary card also shows a 7-day average of 5.8 km). November 15 is the only day with itemised data:

That was the post-trip sodium flush: 28,000 steps, 19.8 km, and the 1.2 kg the dinner added on the 14th came off 0.7 of the way within two days. Walking was a main force behind that.

In maintenance I no longer chase pace or distance. What I want is **movement every day**. Once the wound is fully settled, running comes back — but the daily-walk floor stays. It is unglamorous, and it is the steadiest block in the whole system.

## 11. Three rules for the second half

1. After a trip, eat high-potassium vegetables for a few days and drink plenty of water to flush the sodium. Celery and leafy greens in rotation; the weight comes back by itself within two days.
2. Seal the scale for two days. After a trip the rhythm is off — do not weigh first, wait until it is regular, so water and sodium do not scare you.
3. Occasional lapses are unavoidable; life has things that must be done and moods that must be lived through. But they cannot become frequent. Keep adjusting mindset, life and work — and remember that health outranks everything else.

The goal for the second half is simple: meals back on schedule, exercise coming back gradually, and at the start of December I will lay out the full November report.
